One of the most common questions trade contractors ask when they start running Google Ads is: "How much should I be spending?"

It's a reasonable question with an honest answer that most agencies won't give you: it depends — but not in the vague, hand-wavy way. There are real benchmarks by trade, a simple formula to figure out your number, and clear signals that tell you when to spend more or pull back.

Here's the straight-talk version.

Why "just start with $500/month" is bad advice

The most common advice from agencies and Google's own reps is to start with a small budget and scale up once you "learn." The problem: in most competitive trade markets, $500/month produces so few clicks that you can't learn anything statistically meaningful. You might get 15–20 clicks at $25–35 per click, which could mean 0–2 calls depending on your conversion rate.

That's not enough data to optimize. It's not enough to know if your campaign is working. And it feels like Google Ads "doesn't work" — which is often just a budget-to-market-size mismatch.

The floor for meaningful Google Ads results in most trade industries is $1,000–$1,500/month. Below that, you're paying for a learning experiment, not a lead generation machine.

Industry benchmarks: cost per lead by trade

These ranges are based on typical Google Ads performance across competitive U.S. markets. High-competition metros (LA, NYC, Chicago) skew toward the upper end; mid-size markets often land in the middle.

  • HVAC: $60–$140 per lead
  • Plumbing: $50–$120 per lead
  • Electrical: $45–$110 per lead
  • Roofing: $80–$200 per lead
  • Pest control: $30–$80 per lead
  • Landscaping: $40–$100 per lead
  • Painting: $50–$130 per lead
  • Concrete/masonry: $60–$150 per lead
  • General contracting: $100–$250 per lead

Emergency services (emergency plumber, 24-hour HVAC) tend to have lower CPLs because urgency drives conversion — people don't comparison-shop when their basement is flooding.

The formula: work backwards from jobs, not forwards from budget

Instead of picking a budget and hoping it works, calculate what you need to spend to hit your revenue goal. Here's the formula:

  1. Target additional monthly revenue: How much more do you want to generate from Google Ads? Start with a realistic number — say, $15,000/month in new jobs.
  2. Average job value: What does a typical booked job from an ad lead pay? Let's say $800 for HVAC.
  3. Jobs needed: $15,000 ÷ $800 = ~19 jobs per month.
  4. Leads needed (at your close rate): If you close 40% of leads into booked jobs, you need ~47 leads per month.
  5. Budget needed: At $90 CPL (mid-range for HVAC), that's ~$4,200/month in ad spend.

The ROI check: you're spending $4,200 to generate $15,000. That's a 3.6x return on ad spend — which is solid for service businesses where lifetime customer value is much higher than the first job.

The other side of the equation: what you lose from bad targeting

Budget alone isn't the whole story. A common mistake is pouring money into Google Ads without the right setup — and watching 30–50% of that spend go to completely irrelevant searches.

A roofing company bidding on "roofing" with broad match will get clicks from:

  • "roofing jobs near me" (people looking for work, not a contractor)
  • "roofing materials prices" (DIYers)
  • "how to fix a roof yourself" (definitely not your customer)
  • Competitor brand terms

Every one of those clicks costs you money and converts at near-zero. Fixing the targeting — moving to phrase and exact match, building a robust negative keyword list — typically cuts wasted spend by 20–40% without reducing call volume. That's like getting a 25–65% discount on your current budget.

When to increase your budget

You should increase your Google Ads budget when:

  • Your CPL is below your industry benchmark and you have capacity for more jobs
  • Your conversion rate from lead to booked job is above 40%
  • You're seeing diminishing returns from organic and other channels
  • Competitors are increasing market share in your search terms
  • Seasonality is in your favor (HVAC summer cooling season, roofing after hail season, etc.)

When to pull back

Pull back or pause when:

  • Your CPL is 2x+ above benchmark with no improvement after 30 days of optimization
  • You're at capacity and can't handle more jobs
  • Your close rate from leads is below 20% (this is a sales problem, not an ads problem)
  • You haven't set up call tracking — you're spending blind

One thing that changes the math entirely: continuous optimization

The biggest variable in Google Ads ROI isn't your budget — it's how well your campaigns are managed over time. A well-optimized campaign generating $80 CPL today will drift to $120–$150 CPL within 60–90 days without active management, as bid auctions shift, search behavior changes, and underperforming keywords accumulate spend.
